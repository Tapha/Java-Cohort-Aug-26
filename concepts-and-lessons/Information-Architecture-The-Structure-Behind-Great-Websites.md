# Information Architecture: The Structure Behind Great Websites

The **structure of information** is what makes information useful, navigable and compelling.

It’s no accident that directories were among the first websites to become enormously popular in the early days of the Web.

Yahoo’s original name was **“Jerry and David’s Guide to the World Wide Web.”** It was, essentially, a directory of other websites.

So why does that matter? What does the popularity of directories tell us about websites today?

The answer is **Information Architecture**.

These websites became useful because of the **way they structured information** — the way they organised it to, as Yahoo’s founders originally put it, *guide* you.

The same principles can be applied to your own website.

Let’s break them down.

---

## Principle 1: A Website Is a “Place,” Not a “Thing”

As Alan Kay once remarked:

> “A change of perspective is worth 80 IQ points.”

One reason we often get websites wrong is that we misunderstand what they actually are.

A **thing** is something you use.

A **place** is something you enter, inhabit and move through.

A website should therefore be designed as an environment in which the user is **guided from one state to another**.

---

## Principle 2: Start With the Overall Structure

Begin with the big picture:

* What is the website trying to achieve?
* What is the user trying to achieve?
* What are the top-level sections?
* What should appear in the main navigation?
* How should those sections relate to one another?

Only after establishing this structure should you descend into individual pages, layouts and components.

This is the approach that [@PlanFlowDev](https://twitter.com/PlanFlowDev) helps you take: **structure first, details second.**

---

## Principle 3: Understand the User’s Goal

Imagine your website’s information hierarchy as a pyramid.

At the top is the **goal**.

That goal determines everything underneath it.

If the ultimate goal is to get someone to buy a product, for example, the journey might contain several smaller goals:

1. Understand what the company offers.
2. Browse the available products.
3. Open a product page.
4. Evaluate the product.
5. Read reviews or supporting information.
6. Click **Buy**.

Each of these subgoals is supported by something in the interface:

* pages,
* sections,
* navigation links,
* buttons,
* content,
* components.

Together, these elements create the context in which the overall goal can be achieved.

The architecture of the website is therefore not arbitrary.

It is the **structure of the user's journey toward a goal**.

---

## Principle 4: Build Key Links Around the Goal

Your primary navigation should reflect the most important stages of the user's journey.

If the goal of a website is to get someone to create an account, for example, the main navigation might be:

**Home → Create an Account → Login → Contact Us**

Each link has a role:

* **Home** — general information and orientation.
* **Create an Account** — the primary action.
* **Login** — returning-user access.
* **Contact Us** — support when something goes wrong.

Notice that this is a hierarchy.

The navigation is not simply a collection of useful links. It represents the main routes through the system.

Once those routes are understood, you can begin designing the individual pages.

---

## Principle 5: Structure Page Layouts Around Goal and Hierarchy

Many websites are designed as if each page were an isolated graphic-design composition.

But a page is not merely something to look at.

It is a **visual guide**.

The position, prominence and ordering of elements should communicate:

1. Where am I?
2. What is important here?
3. What can I do?
4. What should I do next?

Remember that the [map is not the territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Map%E2%80%93territory_relation).

A sitemap can describe the broad structure of a website, but it becomes genuinely useful only when that structure is connected to the user's goals.

The same principle applies at every level:

**Goal → hierarchy → navigation → page → section → component → action**

Even websites designed without an explicit Information Architecture process often reproduce parts of this logic accidentally.

Look at almost any conventional website.

What is one of the first structures you encounter?

The **main menu**.

That menu is the visible surface of a deeper information hierarchy.

Understanding these principles means you no longer have to arrive at that hierarchy accidentally.

You can design it deliberately.

And that is one of the foundations of building better websites.
